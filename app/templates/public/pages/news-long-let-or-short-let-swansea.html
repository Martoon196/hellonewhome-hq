Long let or short let in Swansea? The honest maths

Three Welsh rules and one number decide it, and most people giving confident answers sell only one of the two options. We manage both, so here is the working.

Every few weeks someone with a two-bed near the Marina or a cottage on Gower asks: would it make more as a short let? Honestly, it depends. Start with the legal question, not the income question

Use class. Since October 2022 Wales has had three residential use classes: C3 (primary home), C5 (second home) and C6 (short-term let). A property can move between them without planning permission unless the council has made an Article 4 direction, as Gwynedd and Eryri have. We have found none covering short lets in Swansea verify with Swansea Council planning, but a flat's lease or a mortgage may still prohibit nightly letting, and that check comes first.

The 182-day test. Since April 2023 a self-catering property in Wales is only assessed for business rates, rather than council tax, if it is available to let for at least 252 days a year and actually let for at least 182. Fall short and it is council tax, and because a furnished home that is nobody's main residence counts as a second home, Swansea Council adds a 100% premium verify current rate; Welsh law allows up to 300%. The Welsh Government has consulted on multi-year averaging and counting up to 14 charity stays verify legislative status; until that is law, plan on 182.

The register, and possibly a levy. The Wales visitor accommodation register opened on 1 October 2026. Anyone taking paid overnight bookings, including one flat on Airbnb, must register with the Welsh Revenue Authority; existing providers have until 31 March 2027, and new ones must register before their first guest. Councils may add a visitor levy from April 2027 at the earliest (£1.30 per person per night for most accommodation). Swansea Council's last published position was no plans to introduce one verify current position.

If your property clears those three, you can look at the money.

What the short-let figure hides

Occupancy is not a property feature; it is a result of pricing, photography, response times, reviews and the calendar. The same flat run by an owner who sets one price in March and leaves it, and by a team that re-prices nightly around Arena dates and university weekends, will not land on the same number.

Cleaning is passed through to guests, but turnovers are not free: linen, restocking, an inspection between every stay, and the cleaner who does not show at 10am on a Saturday. Utilities move from the contract-holder's name to yours; so does council tax if you miss 182 nights. Reviews compound in both directions.

The 182 nights are not a target you set; they are an outcome you track, because the council will ask.

When a short let wins in Swansea

Flats in SA1 and the Marina with parking, where Arena events, university visits, hospital contractors and Monday-to-Thursday work stays flatten the year; and well-presented Mumbles and Gower properties with a view, where summer rates carry a quiet winter. In both, the owner is realistic about seasonality and the property is furnished for guests, not for the owner's own weekends.

When a long let wins

A terrace in Morriston or Sketty with no parking and an owner who wants the income to arrive on the first of the month. A leasehold flat whose lease forbids short lets. A cottage the owner uses themselves for a month each summer, which quietly eats the 182-day test. Any property where the honest occupancy number is in the thirties.
