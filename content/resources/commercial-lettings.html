A commercial letting is a shop, an office, a workshop, a warehouse or a unit let to a business on a lease. Almost everything you know about letting a home does not apply, and a different set of rules does. This page is the short version of that set, in plain English, with the checklist head office needs before it can appraise a commercial property. It is a summary of the position in England and Wales in October 2026 and it is not legal advice: the landlord's solicitor advises on the lease, and their accountant on VAT and rates. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own commercial property law (no Landlord and Tenant Act 1954 security of tenure, separate EPC, rates and VAT practice), so this page does not describe them.

What does not apply

No deposit scheme

Tenancy deposit protection is a rule for residential tenancies of homes (assured tenancies in England, occupation contracts in Wales). A commercial rent deposit is held under a rent deposit deed, a short document the solicitor drafts alongside the lease. It says how much is held, where the money sits, when the landlord may draw on it (usually unpaid rent or a breach of the lease) and when it is returned. Never tell a commercial landlord their deposit has to go into a scheme.

No Renters' Rights Act

The Renters' Rights Act 2025 governs residential tenancies in England, and the Renting Homes (Wales) Act 2016 does the same in Wales. A business lease is governed by the Landlord and Tenant Act 1954, the lease itself and the general law of contract. So there is no periodic tenancy by default, no written statement of terms in the residential form, no once-a-year rent increase procedure of that kind and no landlord database entry for the property.

No Right to Rent

Right to Rent checks are for adults occupying a home. A business occupier is referenced as a business: filed accounts, trading history, bank and trade references, and a guarantor or a larger rent deposit when the covenant is thin. Head office runs the referencing; the partner collects the company name, number and the name of the person signing.

What does apply

Security of tenure under the Landlord and Tenant Act 1954

By default a business tenant has the right to a new lease when the old one ends, on broadly the same terms. Landlord and tenant can agree to exclude that right (contracting out), but only if the landlord serves the statutory warning notice and the tenant makes the declaration before the lease is signed. The choice goes in the heads of terms; the landlord's solicitor serves the notice. Always ask whether an existing lease is inside or outside the Act, because it changes what the landlord can do when it ends.

MEES for non-domestic property

A non-domestic property has needed an EPC rating of E or better to be let since 1 April 2023, unless an exemption is registered on the PRS Exemptions Register. The government has proposed raising the minimum to C by 2027 and B by 2030. Collect the rating and the certificate expiry on the visit, and note any works the landlord is planning. A property rated F or G cannot be marketed as ready to let.

Business rates and empty-rates relief

The occupier pays business rates once the property is let. An empty property is the owner's liability after the relief period, which is three months for most property and six months for industrial property. A vacant unit is therefore appraised with the rates bill and the letting timetable side by side, and the landlord is told what a month of voids costs them.

VAT option to tax

Commercial rent is exempt from VAT unless the owner has opted to tax the building. Once opted, VAT is charged on the rent and on the service charge and other sums the lease treats as rent (a deposit attracts VAT only when it is drawn on), and some occupiers (charities, financial businesses) cannot recover it, which affects who will take the lease. Whether to opt is a decision for the landlord and their accountant. Find out whether an option is in place; never advise on it.

Code for Leasing Business Premises 2020

The RICS professional statement that sets out how heads of terms are negotiated and recorded: in writing, in plain terms, and covering rent, term, break rights, repairing obligations, rent review, alienation (assignment and subletting) and the 1954 Act position before either side instructs a solicitor. Head office drafts the heads of terms to the Code; the partner's job is to gather what they need.

How the service runs

  1. Appraisal. The partner visits with the checklist below. Head office writes the appraisal: a rent, the lease terms the market will bear, the compliance position and the fee.
  2. Marketing. Particulars with plans and photographs, the commercial portals, a board where it helps, and viewings by the partner.
  3. Heads of terms and referencing. Drafted to the 2020 Code by head office; the business referenced; the deposit or guarantor agreed.
  4. The lease. Drafted and completed by the landlord's solicitor. We do not draft leases and we do not give legal advice.
  5. Rent collection, management and rent reviews. Run from head office under the lease: rent demands, service charge accounts, repairs, insurance, inspections, and the review and renewal dates.

The commercial fee sheet is published on the commercial page, and every commercial fee is quoted plus VAT. Do not quote your own figure on the visit: point the landlord to the published sheet, and head office confirms the fee in writing with the appraisal. Never say "no VAT" about a commercial fee.

The short checklist for the visit

  • Use class and any change of use the landlord wants.
  • Floor area, floor by floor, in square feet or square metres.
  • Lease status: vacant, let or lease ending; break dates; inside or outside the 1954 Act.
  • EPC rating and expiry, and the MEES position.
  • Rateable value and who pays the rates today.
  • Service charge: what is recovered and last year's figure.
  • Fit-out: what is the landlord's and what is the outgoing tenant's.
  • Access, loading, opening hours and shared entrances.
  • Parking and yard space.
  • Planning constraints: listed status, conservation area, Article 4, signage.
  • Photographs of the exterior, every floor and the services, plus a plan.
